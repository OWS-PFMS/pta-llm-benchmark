
Interviewer:  My first question to you is, “In your opinion, what aspect of this network is working well in sustaining water services in the county?

Interviewee: I can say, the full participation of each actor, from the government, to the non-government, even to the community. The full participation. 

I can say, issues on policy, if the relevant agencies, probably the county government or the national government are able to either implement, enhance, or come up with policies that ensure that sustainability of water is there. 

If everyone plays their role, like for example, us Red Cross, if we have funds from the donors and we are able to implement as per what we requested from the donors and that works well, then that ensures that the communities that we are serving have sustainable supply of water.

Interviewer:  My second question to is, “What do you think are the main problems in sustaining water services in the county?

Interviewee: I can say community dependency. For instance, you implement a project, of course a donor-funded project has a time frame, probably we are implementing it within a year, we have trained or given skills to the community on managing, the sub-county water officers come in on training them on managing water kiosks and such…But you see, after the implementing actor pulls out, they feel…the community still feels that there should be people here monitoring us and bringing fuel for the pumps and such. So, sometimes the community itself is a hindrance to the sustainability.

I can’t say that there is anything major, because with the water department, the sub-county water officers, the water directors, I believe they do their duties as is required – of following up the communities and ensuring that if it is water systems, they are working, for instance if we call their offices and tell them that there is a leakage somewhere, it is always taken care of. So, in the rural areas, I must say, it squarely lies with the communities. Probably, there is a gap of letting them know that it is their project; it is helping them. It is not helping the implementing actor, it is not helping the donor, so at the end of the day they must accept it and move with it, whether there is a donor or not, whether there is a government or not.

Interviewer:  My third question to is, “What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems?

Interviewee: Sometimes, the sustainability in the rural areas…(I forgot to mention this) it has always been a challenge…is land ownership issue, because you cannot have a project in the clouds. It has to be somewhere, and areas that are mostly identified are usually land that is owned by one community member, and probably they think that they should be given much in terms of compensation. So…but speaking from where I sit as Red Cross, we engage from the word go. Signing agreements that you have agreed for us to implement a project here you have signed an agreement for these few years or probably that we do a line for you to your house or to you home and then you give the land to the community – that portion of land to the community. So, land ownership has been an issue that has hindered sustainability. 

For instance, we had a case in one of our projects in Kitui Rural where one of the landowners where the pipeline was passing through his shamba (piece of land) so he said…”no you are not going to pass through my piece of land until you do this for me”. He was very adamant and we said this one person cannot really hold a project that is serving more than like 20,000 households. So we said we had to sit down and agree…yes we will put, is it that tee…ok his main complaint was that…”you are putting a plastic pipe on my piece of land, it will break any time why don’t you put another one”…and that was logic and so we had to agree and we called the water officer and we called the sub-county water officer and we agreed and we said then that should be done for the project to continue and it is time-bad. So, issues on land issues have caused sustainability to be an issue.

According to me, the number one solution would be engaging the communities from the word go. We might sit here and decide that people in Kitui South need water, yet according to them that is not their priority. Probably if they are given food or seed, they would be better off. So before we make that judgement of even sitting down and doing a proposal to the donor, because, after all, we have made a proposal to a donor, it has gone through, you cannot change the implementation and say…”we are going to provide beans yet you had indicated that you are providing maize”…. So it is always good for the all actors, including the government itself to engage the communities from the word go. It is a kind of…a need assessment through the community first. Fine, yes we need water, but is it right if we place it at point B or is it right if we place it at point A. Probably, the community itself, when they are engaged from that level, we won’t have even the land issues, because they will say placing it at point B is better than it point B. But maybe because we are going there as technical people, we think we have the knowledge, we do not want to listen to them, we insist on placing it at point A, of course there is someone who will rise…we have the gatekeepers in the communities and tell them …”no, do not allow this to happen”…yet you have already pumped some funds there, the project might stall and such. So in everything, we should involve the community from the word go even before we do the proposals.

I want to believe the involvement can sort everything else, including the land ownership issue…because when you engage them, it is actually them who can tell you that …”we can talk to one of us. Because we think at point A it is better off leave us first, we talk to this person so that he can allow us to…” You see now, you have the good will from the community and you can actually implement easily and within a short time.

Interviewer:  Of the solutions you have mentioned, which do you consider to be the most important? 

Interviewee: Engaging the community.

Interviewer:  If the solution happens, what would occur next?

Interviewee: For instance, it has happened in so many areas and cases and with different organizations and when you meet the people, they tell you…in Kiswahili they call it “mradi” (project). They will alo call it in Kikamba “mulati”. They tell you…”this project is for Kenya Red Cross. This project is for this and this organization”…and that organization has been there maybe for the last 3 years and have already phased out and they are gone and the community is still saying that the project is for Kenya Red Cross. That happens because probably Kenya Red Cross did not engage the community from the word go. We just came, with the machinery and everything, we sat down and probably because we have the social power, (I am not saying that it is Red Cross, it can be any other person). Probably because we have the social power over them, they have to listen to us and we implement. But…we move with them like that then we leave, it is a Red Cross project. Nobody is maintaining it, nobody is really concerned about it so basically there is no much impact to the community they go back to where they are.

I can give an example without the organization. In Embu, an organization constructed toilets at a certain community for community use. The community used the toilets until they were filled up and they were locked up. When you go there and ask them, they tell you…”these are toilets for such an organization”. You know, after the toilets were filled up, they went back to…You see, they go back to the same problem that they had, but probably if they could have been engaged from the word go, they should have known even…because when you are exiting or phasing out, you should link the community with the relevant offices or with the relevant people that will be following up with them. When you are handing over, you are handing over to the community, but ensuring that there is someone who will be there and maybe…and in most cases it will be the government because the government will always be there. Yes. Someone like Red Cross will always be there, but not the donor. We might be having funds today to visit the projects on and off but when the donor funding has ended I will not be able to commute to that particular place, but the government will always…the structure and the systems of the government will always be there. So, you have to link the community either with the government office or with the government official to ensure that there is that continuity. 

I want to believe that when you begin with engaging the community, they move step by step with the project and they own it…they own it completely. So, that ensures sustainability, because it belongs to them when you are there or not. What you can see belongs to them.

Interviewer:  Is there anything you would like to add?

Interviewee: Over and over, the issues I have mentioned have been the main challenges with us, the implementers. We always miss that point of…There was a time back in 2008 or 2010 we had some donors…not really donors, but they gave us some training on community approach and they were calling it “First Impact Methodology”. They dubbed it “First Impact Methodology”…of ensuring that you are engaging the person you are serving from the word go. I learned a lot from that concept because it even applies in day to day life. You know, like when you are relating with your family members, your children, colleagues at work…somebody could be your senior but if they are always pushing files to your desk, whatever is in that file…you were not involved from the word go. It is like just…you do that. The way you will do it is different from the way you have come here and we have gone through this interview step by step. Other than bringing me a questionnaire and leaving it for me to fill and just send back to you. You know I can just write anything on that questionnaire just to fulfill that…you guys have come and I have played my role. But you see, the kind of engagement is different from just pushing something to someone. It squarely applies to implementing projects and programmes in the communities, and that is where we miss the point…I must say, as implementers.
