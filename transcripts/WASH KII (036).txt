Question 1: In your opinion, what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui? Could you please explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability?
Respondent: The biggest challenge in the rural water schemes is the management issue because most of the rural schemes are managed by the community by selected leaders. Most of the youngsters have moved to towns so whoever is selected there most of them are people with low literacy levels and people who are elderly. So, they know very little and as organizations, including our organization, we rarely do follow up of the trainings that we offer from time to time. For sustainability, those groups and the leaders of those groups are supposed to be trained continuously. We train them once and because of minimum funds, we are unable to train them again. We don’t train them continuously and they forget what they have been trained on the same day of the training and when they forget, they do things their own way. They do whatever they think is correct and sometimes it doesn’t work out and so the projects fail and the facilities cannot be used by the community. 
When a project is implemented or constructed, everything is good because the water is flowing. When the breakdowns start, the scheme leaders cannot remember to save or set aside money for that project for sustainability purposes and especially money for repairs to make service delivery sustainable. They then start blaming each other for the failures and this just messes up everything.
The other thing is that when organizations are going to train the scheme managers or the community leaders who are going to manage projects, most of the people elected are youngsters; young men and women who very active. This is because the youth are learned and there is a perception that it should be them to go for the trainings, but you find that whatever they learn in those trainings, they don’t teach the elderly. When the youth come back from the trainings, they come with the mindset that it is their time to get money from the projects. So, they are there thinking that when the water is sold the money should be shared amongst themselves so that they can do their own things. In real sense the money which is collected from selling of water is supposed to be strictly for maintenance of the water facility. That is the understanding most of the groups lack or most of the rural schemes or the leaders lack and this definitely affects sustainability. 
The other challenge which I can mention is climate change. You know most of the rural schemes are boreholes, springs, small dams and water pans, all those are prone to the impacts of climate change because as the dry spell comes most of our borehole water levels go down, making it probably impossible to recharge as fast as it used to when it was drilled. So, the area is becoming dryer as we move on, I don’t know what we are going to about it.
Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems?
Respondent: What I can say can reduce these challenges is continuous training. Organizations can organize for quarterly trainings. At least by the end of one year, management committees will have stabilized in terms of acquiring relevant skills. The youth who might get jobs in urban centres can leave the rural areas and other people at the scheme level can be trained and we will have at least 80% of the rural schemes being stable, sustainable and reliable to the community.
We also need to move away from drilling boreholes and we concentrate on the sub surface dams and establishing dams across the river. Let us focus on surface water because when we do so, we will be recharging the ground and the water levels in the boreholes, even those which have been drilled in some distance the water level will rise. When we continue drilling boreholes, we are depleting the ground water levels which are our reserves. We also need to protect and conserve springs because they are being depleted. 
The other thing is that I have talked about the continuous training. I can also add that the government needs to provide subsidies. You know that sourcing for water and providing it is a very expensive affair. The government should give subsidies to the water schemes. Like during this dry season, you know that everybody is concentrating on the boreholes and whatever dam which is nearby fetching water, not small dams. So, the much we can do with that is the subsidies of water, from the government to the communities like giving them fuel; not money, rehabilitating the projects when they keep breaking down and the community cannot repair them. The other thing is we concentrate on reforestation and restoration of conservation areas. Communities should be encouraged to plant trees around their project sites. That will cater for the climate change.
I think the whole problem of maintenance is related to finance and finance is somehow related to training. When we train them well, they will be able to know which money belongs to the project and which money belongs to their pockets. By knowing this, they will be able to keep money for the project to support operation and maintenance. You know the essence of training the community is to make sure that they can manage the finances from the project well and support with maintenance. When they understand this, they will even open bank accounts. Some of the groups open accounts and after a short time they abandon the accounts. You can bear me witness-if you go around the schemes, none of their accounts have money and if there is, it is very little. If they come to understand that the money is meant for the project and that rural water provision is not a profit-making business, things will improve.

Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: They were all important but I will pick training is the major one. If we continuously train the community and impact knowledge on them, we will be able to reduce breakdowns of the rural water schemes by almost 80% because they will have a clear understanding that the water is not being sold to make a profit but to be able to deliver continuous and reliable service. The schemes will open bank accounts for the projects and so be able to carry out repairs. I know of some groups who are well trained – we trained them together with UNICEF and 7 years down the line, the projects are still functional. Those groups have been able to replace the submersible pump which costs about 200,000 shillings, without external support from a partner or from the county.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: Currently, we have transitioned from being a water board to a Water Works Development Agency (WWDA) whereby our mandate is only infrastructure development. We deal with the rural schemes under funding for programs. The national government is funding us for major infrastructure. So, it is like currently we do not have a service provision element. Initially we were distributing water, managing the projects and giving subsidies to the community. Now we don’t have that mandate. Our core mandate is major infrastructure development for water. We are focusing on increasing the infrastructure, we are not managing systems. We drill boreholes, we construct more pipelines, we rehabilitate existing systems under program funding; like now under the drought program we are rehabilitating the existing systems, drilling some boreholes, providing support for water trucking and providing water tanks. At least we are helping them to revive the projects which are stalled but under a program but it is not our core mandate. When I say program, I mean a number of projects lumped into one large work package. A program can be funded either by the national government or by donors.
Our mandate determines the kind of interventions we implement, and our mandate as I have mentioned is development of big infrastructure. We also set up transboundary water infrastructure which span across counties as well as across countries. We also consult with the county government on the needs for the sector so that we can plan around them and write proposals. We work closely with them and we even hold meetings with them like with this drought mitigation program we are doing. We discussed with them what we were planning on doing and asked them what they think is better is for us to do. We also keep asking them for the projects they would like to be rehabilitated in case a program and funding comes along.
For major infrastructure development we do surveys to determine the situation on the ground and then come up with proposals which we send to the national government which then makes selection on which projects to fund. Just to be clear, we do socio-economic and topographical surveys in order to make proposals. We also do a political analysis in order to advice.
