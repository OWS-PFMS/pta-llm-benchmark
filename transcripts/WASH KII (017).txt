Question 1: In your opinion what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui County? Could you explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability? 
Respondent: Well, I think from my opinion what I can see is that there is a problem with the community themselves managing those water supplies, there is also the problem of the technology they are using. Most of them use diesel generators which are very expensive to maintain. We have ran some of them and we know it's very expensive to maintain. Those are the main challenges I see.
Interviewer: What else?
Respondent: Also there is lack of legal/institutional framework, how to have a system that can be properly monitored, evaluated and people held accountable. So there is that lack of system. For example if a certain community member is entrusted with the responsibility of being a treasurer for example, if he misappropriates that money that is collected, how is he or she held accountable, is there a systemin place to make sure they are held accountable?
Interviewer: So there is the community managing the water structures and then there is the issue of inadequate or lack of institutional framework and then technology. What else?
Respondent: You know the water sector has institutions mandated with various responsibilities and one of them is WASREB, which is basically the regulator. If a way could be found to link these community water supplies to WASREB so that whatever rules and regulations WASREB come up with, they are applied to those community water supplies. The issue of institutional accountability issues will be solved to a large extent.
Interviewer: You mean the community managed water supplies?
Respondent: Yes. So there ought to be some model on how do you bring them into a regulatory regime. Some people have been suggesting that let them be answerable to WASREP through the WSPs. But that model has never come out clearly. There are many models that WASREB has come up with, maybe those people who are keen on making this community water supplies work can explore and see which ones can give the results we want.
Interviewer: What other challenge? You talked about community managing  the water supply systems, what is the problem with that?
Respondent: Basically it's the issue of accountability and also the issue of the tariff they are using. Maybe those tariffs are not adequate to meet their expenses. And now vis-à-vis the cost of operating those facilities. So there could be that problem. People mostly have been talking of the management issue, but you know management is a wide area. You could be weak in one area and strong in another but the key things I can see is one, how people account for the money, the revenue they get from the water sales. The second one is tariffs, the tariffs they use are they based on anything, because ideally they should be base on the inputs, how much do you use to produce say, 1m3 and when you sell, how much are you getting so that you see whether you are able to break even and have something for any break down or any need in future. And also there is the, when these community water projects come into being, you see non-governmental and others, they go into the community, they bring up a project, everybody is happy, it is new, communities are trained and they are told go on. But somewhere along the way they start failing then they run to the ministries, the government for help and they get the help sometimes they don't get sometimes. So there is that mentality that the community water supplies are supposed to be helped by the government. So the issue of sustainability is not internalized. When they are running well, okay, when things start going wrong they run to the government for the support. And I think that's also part of the problem where people think sustainability is not an issue to think seriously about. 
Interviewer: Any other challenge that you think is really contributing to lack of sustainability.
Respondent: Probably you know when you talk of rural there are many hardships. Infrastructure probably, you find maybe we may say it is good to have power from the grid but the power has not reached there, maybe you want to change from diesel to solar but they don't have the resources to do that, all those things are factors to consider. 

Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems?  
Respondent: First is to explore the models WASREB has come up with and see which one can be applied to a particular region. Regions are different, they have different challenges but out of the different number of models they have, one or two may be applicable in a particular region. So if that can be explored, that should be the starting point. Where you want system in place for the managing, the utilities and being able to hold people accountable for the purpose of sustainability. Because at the end of it if they were sustainable, if a community water project came up and it is sustainable, you see the problem is solved because one, the water services will continue and they will not depend on other people for them to continue operating.
Interviewer: On that point of the management models, I believe you were part of the policy development though it has not come into full implementation, and during policy development you suggested some management models, do you think when the policy comes into place will address fully the issue of management or do you have any other suggestions on the model of management of these facilities?
Respondent: To a large extent it will but it should zero in on that aspect of which model for particular community water supplies because a policy covers even the established WSPs, the water policy but then there are those particular aspects of that water policy that are towards solving the problems of community water projects. So those ones need to be understood by those people who want to bring up the community water projects that can work. The other one is using the appropriate technology when coming up with those projects and those that are already existing we can upgrade. We can have like hybrid system where you have a diesel power source and you can also have solar. Tariffs also need to be thought through because more often than not people will just say this water will be sold at two shillings but is it based on any study to know that what's the operational cost of this project so that when you decide it two shillings you know it will cover the entire cost. So tariffs should also be based on a study about the operations of that particular facility.
Interviewer: So you are proposing a study to identify which tariffs are best for water services?
Respondent: For a particular, because they may not be the same across the board, the cost could vary depending on the source of power, and the area of coverage. And you know also, another aspect that comes into mind is the ability to pay. That will always come up especially in the rural areas but you know once you have known that it takes Kshs10 to produce1m3 of water and you find that by selling Kshs2 you won't be able to make that Kshs10, can you establish that gap and know, who will chip in to fill that.
Interviewer: Any other idea to the challenges that you mentioned?
Respondent: Another one is collaboration maybe when those water supplies are in place and running there ought to be a long-term relationship with the other institutions, other interested parties so that that support is a continuous process. Like I've heard on a few areas, meetings I've attended, I've always heard the sentiment that some of the NGOs come, they develop very good projects and when they pull out their projects collapse. Maybe probably if they pulled out could have linked these community water supplies with some other institutions so that there is a continuing relationship that could help. 
Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps will happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: To me the most important is to address the issue of the institutional/legal framework under which these water supplies operate. So deciding which model to use and anchoring it in law, to me is the most important. 
Interviewer: So with the institutional and legal framework, so what?
Respondent: Once that is in place, you are sure that you can be able now to monitor how those water supplies are run and to a large you can be able to put in place the management structure that will be held accountable to make sure that facility continues to operate.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: You know actually we are more of urban water supplies than rural. We only run four boreholes which are in the rural areas and we operate them centrally. We have some staff working there and we check the books, they account for the revenue, that's the way we operate. We don't really get involved with the real community water supplies. We also do water trucking. Those people who are not able to access our water through the pipework, we water truck.
Interviewer: So, what influenced those decisions?
Respondent:  It is need driven. It is need driven in the sense that, for example water trucking, there are many people who are outside our distribution network and they always used to come to us saying they want water. So we decided to ask Tanathi to provide us with some water trucks and they have helped a lot because we are able to truck water to those people. About the boreholes, Tanathi being the services board in charge of this area realised there was a problem with those boreholes. They were being managed by communities and the problems were too persistent they decided to come and after understanding the problems there they decided let these boreholes be run by KIMWASCO so they were just handed over to us.
Question 5: How has participating in the WASH forum influenced your organization’s interventions?
Respondent: One the forum has helped us in terms of information. In those forums we are able to know who is doing what and that way we are aware of what is happening even where we have not reached. So I would say it helped a lot in terms of information sharing but where we have not reached is where now to use that information to change things but I think we are heading there with the water policy most of the people who were in the water policy were also attending that WASH Forum so I think most of the input into that water policy was informed by most of the things that were discussed in the water forum.
