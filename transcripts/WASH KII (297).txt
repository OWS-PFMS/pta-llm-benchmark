Question 1: In your opinion, what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui? Could you please explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability?
Respondent: From the perspective of  maintenance service provider, I think number one would be functionality and so you have high non-functionality for equipped sources and by that I refer to pipe schemes and hand pumps, and the effect of high non-functionality on sustainability is the fact that then you still have a section of the county population you know, lagging behind basically with no access as much as the county continues to invest in new water supplies, you are almost guaranteed that after three or four years then you still have a chunk of that population that is newly served falling back to the use of I don’t know unimproved sources or no service. 
Other than functionality then there are governance issues in the sector. By governance I mean performance management is very weak, accountability is very weak, monitoring is very weak and just go to through each of those…so for performance management there is no strong mechanism that holds the service provider into account, I would say both rural and urban. We have all heard that the water service providers (WSPs) in the county are receiving subsidies from the County government whereas there are clear under performance issues like high non-revenue water, poor revenue collection, network mismanagement and all of that. So, because of lack of performance management then no one is held into account for poor service delivery so if there is a breakdown or there is an interruption of service then it’s up to the service provider to decide when to get the issues sorted out and there is no one to tell them that you know if you don’t achieve these targets then these are the implications. Then I think that leads to monitoring because I think the county sector relies heavily on the national regulator for monitoring and I don’t hear them talk about performance of the urban WSPs, leave alone the rural ones. You would understand that the rural ones are very many and it would be difficult to put in place a system to monitor performance. Even the urban ones never talk about performance monitoring – they never present indicators in the Forum. Yesterday, for the first time, I saw indicators from one of the WSPs. Some WSPs do not want to discuss performance and then there is no structure that allows them to be reporting monthly or quarterly to the director on what they have done whereas that should be the case since they are 100% owned by the county so they should be providing reports on their performance. So, if it’s not happening in the urban sector then even in the rural sector it’s not going to happen. 
As regards accountability, I think that links to poor performance management so if I use the example of rural schemes, I don’t have a lot of data on the urban ones. It goes to the issues of financial management and being able to account for services, performance and income to users, for instance you have committees who just do what they want, there is no transparency at all and you cannot tell what happens in most of these schemes. You have water managers by that I refer to the management committees I think it’s the same case as the board of directors for urban water companies and the management who can basically mismanage or misuse finances or resources of the institution and no one really holds them to account so because of that then everyone does what they want and gets away with it which I think is a poor accountability issue. 
I don’t think there are capacity challenges as it’s often said. We have worked with committees that sort of bring on board local teachers and those are the people involved in the management of the rural schemes and what we have seen is that…you know teachers are people who are knowledgeable, very knowledgeable in terms of keeping proper books of account but even where you have some of those people you still struggle a lot with things like financial management and even the schools are some of the poorest payers of water services. So, it is not capacity or lack of understanding, it is largely an accountability issue – the feeling is that you can do what you want and get away with it and of course this leads to corruption that goes on in the wider country. 
I think that the lack of professionalization especially in the rural water sector is a key challenge and I think NGOs and the county have thought that they can fill that gap by training the community, which I don’t think works. The committees have been trained so many times by different actors but the challenges of poor scheme management exist and these affect service delivery to a very large extent.

Then the other biggest challenge in the sector is financing of course both for new systems and existing systems. One of the things I have noticed in the urban side is that whenever they are asked about their poor performance, they say that they are operating poor networks. It is the role of the county to replace the networks but county invests zero in the network replacement which is their role so they go quiet and it becomes an exchange which will never resolve the issue so I think the water sector is underfunded. I would say that the county hasn’t really assumed the role of putting resources in where they are responsible. Issues around asset renewal, asset replacement, asset extensions…I think that is very weak at the moment and I think because of that in the urban side you have networks that are very old, dilapidated and leave a leeway fort water managers or water utilities to say that they are operating a network that is not optimum. For the rural side, they set up systems, set a tariff of 3 shillings and of course there are limitations. The cost of operating the systems are higher and then there are limitations to the amount of tariff you can set. I think they have ended up working with a random figure of 3 shillings and then no one goes on to facilitate the discussion on what is the true cost of running these systems. If you have set a tariff of 3 shillings, then who covers the deficit?

Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these challenges?
Respondent: I would say that I think part of the challenges in the sector are due to the fact that the sector has tended to over rely on the community management model. Everyone, especially NGOs, they are very much focused on that and are not open to new solutions and new approaches for operation and maintenance of schemes. It has become like a religion; they do a system, train the community, hand over the facility to the community, the community handles but they don’t realize that the roles and responsibilities of the community should perhaps be different. It is not a matter of switching on the pumps and running the pumps and it’s not an issue of capacity building of the community and training them to be pump operators; it doesn’t work like that. The right way would be to have communities take up their role as users who can then hold service providers into account. In such a case, then you need someone to come in and fill in the role of a service provider which could be the WSPs, private enterprises or local young men who are graduates; you know, someone who has knowledge of water. Training a community to be a service provider or a pump operator is never going to work. It is not a capacity issue I think it is a skills and professionalization issue. 
There is need for wider discussions around financing because in the rural side and now from a maintenance service provider perspective, there is need for the county to supplement user payments because the tariffs charged are not sufficient for most of the schemes. There are tariffs that can be set to cover the costs, but with those other issues around accountability, poor governance and all that then it’s difficult to even tell how much they make and how much they sell because of those issues so there is a need for discussion on financing. The practice has been that in the urban water sector, the tariffs cover the cost of operation and maintenance and then the county comes in to cover capital expenditure whether it’s asset replacement or extension. So, in the rural side again I think we need to see more of the county government in terms of assuming those roles. I think there is the expectation that 3 shillings per 20-liter jerrican can cover costs of operation and maintenance and extensions and also asset replacement. If a pump burns out then it is not possible to cover costs of replacement. So, I think people need to have an honest discussion on sector financing.
On poor performance management, as I highlighted earlier, there should be clear and proper systems of monitoring and reporting on performance for both the rural and urban sides. 
Interviewer: Under governance you talked about users not being aware of the expected service levels, how do you this can be addressed?
Respondent: I think it falls back to the county government. The county government would need to strengthen the structure of the sector so number one the county government needs to have indicators and measure performance of these water systems  and on the other hand still the county government needs to have a mechanism that allows users to know their rights to agitate for their rights and to escalate their issues to the county government. So, if you are building a performance management system on one side and strengthening the consumer voice on the other end I think you end up  with the WSPs having to provide quality services, and then if they don’t do that then you have a feedback mechanism that then gets the information to the county government but currently because there are no indicators or there are no performance targets for the rural water supplies, then there is no pressure on the service providers to perform, so users don’t even know who to go to, they have no linkages to the county government, they don’t know who to talk to at the county government and the water service provider does what they want so services continue being poor.
Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: I think I would not rank those per se I would say what we need is a vibrant or a robust county policy that then takes care of all those things. Basically, a policy that tries to change the sector and the various areas of weakness and then under the policy you move to rolling out the various initiatives that are built in. I think a policy would be to me the best way forward. By policy I mean a water bill/Act, a water policy and regulations that build in financing, monitoring, in performance monitoring, accountability and governance. Monitoring and performance management would come give the information that you need to understand where are the gaps in the sector and with that then you can be able to resolve issues, to have a discussion on the issues around financing, then with financing you can be able to resolve issues around accountability. 
I think that the county would need to rethink its current practice; the entire design of the system needs to change and of course we are working on that in another project. It is mostly a policy issue because if you think of overreliance on the community management model for instance, you have the discussion with the county government and they say…”you know we don’t have space enough for that in our bill for instance to bring in a private operator to run a scheme immediately it’s built, there are clauses here and there that they can lean on but I think it’s not built in as a county policy that we will do this moving forward...” You look at what actors are doing in terms of projects and handing over to communities and you know it’s going to take a bit more tightening of the sector so number one, how do we create a register of everyone working here, how do we make sure they work through the county so that they implement the county vision and be supportive to the county direction as opposed to going out there and doing what they think is right. However, much community management could work for some areas, when they come to Kitui the focus should be supporting the county’s vision so and the way to do that I think is through policy not necessarily preaching to the NGO’s that this needs to change.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: We are a social enterprise providing repair and maintenance services and our work was informed by research on functionality of water points. It started off as research into monitoring functionality of hand pumps so the University of Oxford did some work on sensors that are fixed on hand pumps and relay information via GSM on functionality and water usage. Through this, it was very easy to know how many days in a year a pump was working or not working. This then evolved into a partnership with UNICEF to trial and understand the cost of maintaining a pump in order to keep a source working. A number of pumps were recruited in Kitui and Kwale and were provided with a free maintenance service for I think one year. The essence of the free maintenance service was to understand the cost of labor, the cost of transport, the economics involved, the parts involved, the cost of the parts and so on. On that basis it was easy to understand the number of breakdowns in a year a regular pump experiences and the cost of getting it up and running and the duration in terms of time and the cost in terms of parts and all that so with that then the next question was then how can we make this happen now that we have the information. So that’s how FundiFix came about and so a payment system was piloted. Based on those understood costs, pumps were to subscribe for maintenance services if they would be willing to pay a fee every month, a flat fee in exchange for unlimited repair and maintenance services. So, FundiFix came about as a result of research on functionality essentially and so we began repairing pumps first, I think from 2014/15 so repairing hand pumps first and then with time we were exhausting the available market for pumps so the question was then what next so we started  working with piped systems in mid-2017. Currently in Kitui we only work in Mwingi North because FundiFix evolved from a research activity in Mwingi North so much of the data on pumps, their functionality, their reliability, household preferences and payment patterns and water sources all that work had been done in Mwingi North so it provided a safe place or rather a good base to set up. If you are moving to new areas, you start mapping out the hand pumps and this costs money and trying to start conversations with the communities to understand them that also costs a lot. So that’s how we started out in Mwingi North and because of providing repair and maintenance for about two years now we have been able to understand more issues around service provision. I think major issues are related to financing and payment for the service. So, our service mainly has remained restricted to Mwingi North and we don’t plan to expand because of financing of the maintenance service. Initially the design was that FundiFix would provide the service and communities would pay for the service and with expansion you know with scale up then the working ratios would improve with time to reduce the need for subsidies but what happened is that as we are providing the service, the payment efficiency is roughly 40%. The revenue collection efficiency is roughly 40% and working ratios around 15% so for every single scheme we sign up then we are taking a 60% hit on revenues; we are not able to collect 60% of what we bill so that impacts our bottom line. An expansion does not necessarily add value in fact it eats into our books and into our other resources and so because of that we have restricted our operations to Mwingi North until we can be able to address the financing issues. There are two financing issues: one of them is accountability at the scheme level or the one I talked about so if we bill a scheme roughly 30% of their water sales, that means they retain 70% so if we bill a scheme 30% how do we make sure that we collect the entire amount and not just 15% out of that 30% so there is a  governance issue and the monitoring issues and performance management issues that I talked about because no one moves to these schemes to audit them and no one locks them up in police cells for misappropriating the money so then they don't pay us. The implication is that we have a low revenue collection efficiency and so we are not able to cover our cost. The last issue is financing in relation to rural water sector: so, sector financing. Even if we were to collect 100% of what we bill communities, I don’t think our working ratios would go over 50% even if we were to do the best job on earth in terms of revenue collection, I don't think our cost coverage would get to 50% - it is even high for Mwingi North. In Mwingi North probably the highest we could hit is probably 40% and if we moved to the entire county, I don't think it would cross the 60% mark and so it tells you that the money we can collect from users cannot pay for the full cost of the service. We still need subsidies and it's only that the level of subsidies would reduce if we expand to new areas and if we collect 100% of the revenues so that has been the second limitation why we are not moving outside Mwingi North, why we are not expanding. We need a discussion around providing subsidies for maintenance service provision because user payments alone and community payments alone are not going to be enough to finance that unless we collect every single coin the community bills which is not possible because they have to pay the kiosk attendant, the pump operators, they have to pay committee allowances, they have to buy fuel, pay night watchmen and so the money they collect cannot go 100% to maintenance alone so then someone needs to come in and supplement that and it is the county government. So, we need to have a discussion around how…actually a good model would be the county government contracting maintenance service providers to keep the sources working. That way, then we have guaranteed financing, job and it even reduces the transaction costs of pushing the communities even to pay when part of the money is not available for whatever reason.
As part of our work, we have designed monitoring logs; we have printed them into hard copies, and distributed them and we have shown them how to fill them out. We have shown and the kiosk operators how to fill them out in fact we have logs for the pump operator, the kiosk attendant and the committee and we have shown them to fill them. We have also included templates that they can use to bill private connections, but it comes back to the issue of accountability and governance because you know the kiosk attendants are used to milk the money and the committees of course misappropriate resources and they don’t fill the logs because it will show where the money is going. Without filing the logs then, the status quo means then someone can just dip their hands in the basket and get away without any evidence or without anyone asking. We have demonstrated how it would work but I wouldn’t say that we have gotten to where we wanted. I would give it a success rate of like 50%.  In fact, none of the committees fill the logs and that tells you that’s where the most leaks are. I think it requires the other bits of the sector weaknesses to be addressed for it to work. If there was someone for example the sub-county water officer going round, you know jailing or locking up people who have stolen scheme money or kicking out committees that are not filling the logs then whenever we introduce the logs people would fill them but because we are introducing the logs and no one asks the committees for the data, checks the records or kicks them out for mismanagement, then it becomes a loop hole, a weak link so it’s not working. So, the other bits of the sector need to be strengthened at the same time.
