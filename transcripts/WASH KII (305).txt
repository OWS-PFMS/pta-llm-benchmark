Question 1: In your opinion, what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui? Could you please explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability?
Respondent: First and foremost, I think it is the issue of planning and budgeting. From the point of view of established water service providers (WSP) like with our company, you will find we have infrastructure that was put in place more than 20 years ago but since there has been no plan to renew, usually when new projects come up they are budgeted for but the existing infrastructure there is no plan or even allocation of resources to rehabilitate and make sure they are renewed to cope with the growing demand.
In terms of the rural coverage, you know the way this project is designed, we have our source as a dam which is about 70 km from here and all along the pipeline we have various lines serving the rural communities and we also have a few boreholes outside the normal network, which we also manage. When it comes to rural projects, they are a challenge for a WSP like us; whereas our operations are 80% focused on urban we find also we are serving rural and it becomes very difficult to manage them well. We are outstretched unless capacity is built so that we have sort of regional offices to manage those rural schemes. You see for the boreholes we manage them centrally and it is costly. Actually, for example if a breakdown occurs, we have to drive all the way to the boreholes which are located very far away from our centre of operations; like the one in Ukasi which is located 70 km away, it is challenging.
Another challenge is the management model for the rural schemes. All of us know that it has been a problem where communities are given a project, they are trained and resources availed. They run them for a while and when they start breaking down, they cannot sustain them so there is need to think of a management model that can work because more often than not communities seem like they lack that organizational skill or set up to be able to manage those schemes properly.
Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these challenges?
Respondent: One that comes to mind is that if the established WSPs can have the capacity both institutional and financial to be able to manage those schemes it may work and probably you see if it’s a big WSP and it has resources and the institutional strength in terms of personnel, organization and equipment and everything they can be able to have some localized managers who are reporting centrally to the head office, that’s a model that I think can work. It will require building the existing companies so that they have that institutional capacity. I know there are other models that the Water Services Regulatory Board (WASREB) has come up with and they can all be explored. 
As you know, provision of water services is an expensive undertaking and no company on its own can be able to develop infrastructure, manage them and give the services so the government comes in. The government cannot detach itself from supporting the business of water service provision so when the government is planning there ought to be resources allocated to the water companies. Although they are independent, but the business of water provision is very expensive. It cannot be left to the private sector or to any other institution so the government ought to plan and budget, funds to be channeled through the WSPs to develop the infrastructure and also to build the capacities to deliver the services but you see the current set up, the companies are created under the companies Act and the Water Act 2002 which has been repealed to the Water Act 2016 so they plan their own budget and everything, when it comes to the government anytime for example a company puts up a request for example that we want to put up a pipeline from a point A to point B, we need these funds, you see the government says… “how can we fund that and you are a private company?” So, you see the private company is left to deal with its issues. The kind of projects that need to be put in place to improve the services are very costly and the companies cannot have those resources so there is some missing link which the legal framework would be able to address because we know now the county governments are supposed to recognize the entities-that is the WSPs. Whether it is a private company or not, it is a county entity providing services on behalf of the county. So, if the legal arrangement allows for the county to plan and budget resources to be utilized by the companies, to a large extend that problem will be solved.
Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: I think we are talking from a point of view of rural water schemes and one of the solutions I have given is where they are managed centrally by the established water companies. So if the capacity of these companies is built, what probably will happen is that they will manage all the water schemes within their area of jurisdiction and like those community schemes from now management point of view internally companies will want to cluster them so that they have a central office or a satellite office with somebody to oversee the operations of those schemes. That’s what I foresee if that happens but then as I said there are so many other models which could be explored, whichever the stakeholders feel will work, well and good because all of us want that problem to be solved.
If the management model is changed from the community-based model, it will translate to those schemes being managed properly, the issue of collecting data and information and the issue of accounting for finances will be dealt with. You see, when the WSP is for example audited, all the operations under WSP are audited so the issue of mismanagement will be completely minimized because if it occurs there will be consequences. This time it is occurring because we have nobody to hold accountable the community management and they are not formal organizations and as such there is really nobody to hold them accountable and their reporting and information sharing is not transparent or even existing.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: We are a WSP and so we provide water through our pipe network, we operate several boreholes and we also do water trucking to communities who live in areas which are not covered by our network. Initially, the Water Act 2002 stipulated that WSPs should only provide services so the government develops assets, it hands them over to the WSPs to manage so we didn’t have the resources to develop the assets and even in our tariffs that are approved by WASREB, there is no component for infrastructure development so they look at it what is your operation and maintenance, so any tariffs when they approve it is focused on those expenses that are related to operation and maintenance but the Water Act 2016 has changed that, they have now provided for companies to develop assets and infrastructure on behalf of the County government. However, we have not done this yet, that is developing infrastructure.
Our interventions are as per our mandate and also demand driven; where we find for example there is a shopping Centre, it has people and there is no water especially during the drought season we truck water there in partnership with the county government and initially the water services board to which we have been reporting before the reforms brought about by the Water Act 2016. The water services board realized that for the community boreholes that we are currently running, the communities were running them down and they were giving them problems so they came and told us to take over management of the community boreholes so we took them but half-heartedly. We took them half-heartedly because of the challenges that the water services board highlighted to us. They told us that the communities were always going to the board to get funds for fuel for the generators and that whenever something broke down, they would run to the board for helps and so they wanted us to take over. We were surprised to be the ones to take over the schemes yet the board has more resources than we do. So far, we have taken over management of 4 boreholes and having operated them for so long, we know they are draining. Actually, we are subsidizing them from the revenues we get from the piped network. If we do analysis, we find that they are making loses so our policy is that even as new community schemes want to be managed by the WSPs, our approach will be that any scheme that is diesel operated it is not acceptable. We would like a scheme that is either connected to the national grid or a hybrid or just solar.
