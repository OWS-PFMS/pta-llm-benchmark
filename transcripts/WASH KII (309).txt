Question 1: In your opinion, what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui? Could you please explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability?
Respondent: In my own perspective, one of the challenges of rural water schemes is that there is lack of transparency from the communities; accountability is an issue whereby the money they get from the sale of water is not accounted for and when it is not accounted for there is dependency syndrome. We have seen some water schemes coming to the county government to ask for fuel support yet they are selling water. The other thing is that there is political influence.  When they fail to account for the water of course the service has been hindered with. As a result, because people don’t have water, they hide themselves behind the politics which are coming up. People don’t have water, it being our responsibility it has to revert back to us. The other thing is in regard to the cost of selling water. The tariffs some schemes are selling at 2 shillings and others at 3 shillings per jerrican yet their systems might require higher tariffs to be able to cover the operation costs, especially pumping costs and also to enable them to make asset and equipment replacement at the facilities.
The other challenge which I have seen is that there is a lot of vested interest in the operation of the schemes and as a result it hinders issues of sustainability whereby there is a lot of turnover in terms of committee management. Whoever were trained leave and others come in, and they may not know how to manage and operate the facilities, they don’t know what is expected of them-the different roles of the different people so you find that some are doubling their roles and that becomes an issue. There is also limited financial support in terms of capacity building. The turnover is as a result of directives in their constitutions, because there constitutions say that committees will serve for three years and then after three years, new people/committees are elected. You see, each new committee needs to be trained yet there is limited budget for that. We can’t cover, in terms of training, over 600 schemes; that becomes a challenge. Along with that, succession plans do not exist within the communities. They clear everybody from the committees because they want to get new blood yet there is also the institutional memory that needs to be kept. There is also limited skills to diagnose what might be the preliminary diagnoses, and the community is not able to do. Though we trained the water resource persons, they have been rejected by the community and some of them did not grasp everything they were trained on. 
Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems?
Respondent: One of the things is the Policy that we are developing and the Bill. They will be able to sort out most of these things because if it is the issue of accountability, maybe there could be a model which is could be suggested, such that now we are not giving this burden to communities-we are not tempting them with money. This could offer a solution. This will also offer solution to the issue of political influence because you cannot politic when there is a clear law which gives legal direction. I think most of them will be addressed under that. The other thing is, because I know whichever way water needs to be subsidized in different forms, there is the issue of maybe financing for the capital equipment.  We need to have a contract framework with some companies so that whenever there is a breakdown, we don’t have to follow the bureaucratic way. This will make it such that there is a softer way of dealing with the time it takes to make repairs and asset replacement following the bureaucratic procedures. The equipment can be procured, repairs done then the payments can follow. The other thing is to have some strategic reserves with us, then we can be able to replace whenever there is a problem. Basically, most of the challenges will be addressed through these means I have mentioned.
Interviewer: You also mentioned limited financial support for capacity building. How can this be handled?
Respondent: Yes, if we develop a trust deed which looks into that, maybe the government creates a kitty or fund that can have partners and other people pool resources together for that purpose then it can also help. As much as we are saying we need to have different water supply and management models, we cannot eliminate communities to some extend because there are schemes that may not fit in any model because of their size. Such schemes need to remain in the community-based management model and so they also need to be trained. The other thing, the government has to take interest also, because I have realized we are planning but there is limited focus in training or capacity building.
Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: I think to me, the Water Bill would be most important because with a Bill, it gives directions, it has regulations, it has everything in it. If we talk about capacity building, and it is embedded in the legal documents, then there will be direction on how to implement and finance it. If it is an issue of politics, or issues of transparency at the scheme level, these can be addressed with different management models captured in the documents.  If this  occurs, what I am foreseeing is that people will enjoy service delivery, they will stop walking long distances, school enrollment and education levels will improve, income levels will increase because the time people spend getting water will be used for other purposes, maybe tending to their farms and businesses will arise, hence development.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: Currently we have two teams, one in Mwingi and the other based here at the headquarters, Kitui. The teams are dealing with issues of diagnosis of broken-down water supplies and also repairing them. We are also purchasing some of the capital equipment to replace in those boreholes that have broken down. There is also a bit of capacity building that we are doing at county level. Officer are looking at the books of the community’s schemes then they are advising us appropriately on how to handle some of the issues and challenges identified in running the schemes. We are also doing monitoring but at times. Not that it should necessarily be funded but it is our daily obligation as a department.
Interviewer: What would you say has informed these interventions? 
Respondent: I think it is just on a need’s basis. Ideally, overtime when you set up infrastructure, it ages then you have to have a plan on how to repair it once it is broken and I think that is how the department was created so that it can respond to the needs as they arise.
