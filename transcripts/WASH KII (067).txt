
Interviewer:  My first question to you is, “In your opinion, what aspect of this network is working well in sustaining water services in the county?

Interviewee: I would think it started with these kind of relationships, that is at the County level, where we came in started working with the county, and understanding their challenges and even the way they work and they way they operate.  So that approach actually helped, because now we were able to easily get into understanding our beneficiaries, who are the community water projects.  So I think that one is working very well.  I think will come to the challenges later, but it’s an important relationship.  Because if we did not have that relationship, we would have a lot of challenges even in all these other aspects.  So that one is important and a good relationship. 

Second is also that through that we got a very good relationship with the community schemes.  And it starts with the management committees of the schemes, and I think one of the best success stories is that when we came in actually we did a lot of information gathering, we did some gap analysis, and we started with the trainings. So from what I see, most projects they actually start the other way sometimes – maybe with infrastructure support and then come with the training component later, to look at sustainability.  But I think KIWASH, it was maybe not even by design, because us we were hired to do training and capacity building, and then there were guys who were hired to do infrastructure.  That’s how it was structured from when it was started.  But once we started like after now, maybe 1 or 2 years, they start to see now integration is very important.  Because the infrastructure guy now sitting in Nairobi, because they’re not seated here, were maybe supposed to do like analysis of the contractor and the physical infrastructure aspect does not really understand the community dynamics, which are very important in implementing some of these projects.  So they found out that the guys who were dealing with the projects one on one from here, were actually named champions, for each project they were appointed champions.  And these champions, now they’re responsibility is cross-cutting: you’re supposed to support the infrastructure team, you’re supposed to support what is called the grants team – who are the ones who look at documentation for the financing of these projects, you’re supposed to support now in training also.  So when we started with the training aspect, and understanding the sub-county and county dynamics, if helped us first of all to even identify the projects we could support, because that is also important: not all projects are good.  That is sometimes a misconception, that you can work with all projects.  There are some projects actually you can’t work with – maybe you have to do something different with them, or they need to sort themselves out before you start working with them.  They’ll just be a failed investment.  So at least we’re able to see which ones we’re able to work with.  Sometimes you might be surprised how few they are.  But when we are working with them, some of them started to improve and others just became better.   So, that understanding of the projects, and from there now bringing in the infrastructure support, in terms of now financing, because we have started already training them, and as you are continuing training them you are understanding things like they don’t know who Water Resource Management Authority (WRMA) is, they don’t know who Tanathi is, they don’t know what the responsibility of the county is to them, so it’s not just a matter of training – actually, we call it coaching and mentorship, so you’re one on one.  Once you do a training, you go on the ground, you see are they implementing, have they gotten the documentation, so that helped.  So when infrastructure came in, and USAID was saying “you know these guys don’t have these documentation”, we could easily go and tell them that “you know we trained you on these, have you gotten these?” “yes, we’ve have tried to get, and these are the challenges we are getting” “do you have these?” “yes, these we have, these we don’t have”.   So those are the projects that actually were picked up very fast and they were successes, just because of the kind of relationship we had already built with them.  So it’s more of a combined effort, where you have to do software, and then hardware, software still even continuous after hardware, so it’s not like they have to be separated, software has to go all the through in different aspects, not just training.  Because software has that component of training, it has that component of coaching, it also has that component of regulation and documentation, which is important.  And then that links now to infrastructure.  So with that now you get success.  And after that now, after the infrastructure, or after the improvement of these services in terms of the hardware component, then now you look at management.  As KIWASH, we have not yet gotten to that yet, where we can say in this one we have supported infrastructure, and now we are looking at management – better management, but maybe in the next few months we’ll some success in that -   it’s still in the initial stages.    But in terms of the change we’ve seen, the ones where we’ve gotten a good relationship and understanding, are the  ones who are the best in terms of hardware support.  But the ones who fell by the wayside – it’s also some kind of personal initiative from them as community members.  I can give an example: if you’re training a community project, and you tell them to do something simple like a business plan in a training on financial business management, if someone is going to draft something that is very rough and say “this is my business plan” you go back to them and say “this is not a proper one, we trained you on this, these are the components that need to be there” but they’re not interested in doing the business plan.  So you see that they’re not serious, so it’s not a person who you’re going to give say half a million dollars for infrastructure – it’s going to be a failed investment from the word go.  So you look at the person you gave a business plan, you gave a template, they did something good, you revised it with them, it actually encompasses what they need to do when it comes to infrastructure.  So it becomes like a proposal. So when the guys from grants for infrastructure come in, you tell them “this is what we’ve been training, these are their needs, that is what they want”.  Now the technical guys from KIWASH can come on the ground and see what is feasible and what is not feasible from their proposal.  But already you have a community which is engaging and really has initiative.  But if you work with a community that lacks initiative, then you’re doomed from the word go.  

 Interviewer:  So what I’ve gathered from you is that the approach that KIWASH is using in linking from the county to the community – you’ve seen it work, and you’ve highlighted part of that.  What I’m interested in is how about the other actors in your map?  How is what they’re doing and what you’re doing working well to improve or to increase the likelihood of sustainability overall in water services?

Interviewee: Ok, I’ll start with the sub-contractors also when it comes to both the consulting, and the hardware and software.  You have to have good contractors – I’ll call them contractors because that’s the term we use.  You know in Kenya when you say contractors, it’s for hardware, but for KIWASH when we say contractors, it’s for both hardware and software.  So a contractor is just like any partner you’ll bring in to support you, and it can be both hardware and software so you need to have quality contractors – they add a lot of value to the work you do.   So, if you don’t get good contractors, then at the end of the day, because as KIWASH we don’t have the capacity in terms of human resources.  Kitui is a big county, so we need good contractors to supervise the work that is being done and to do also good work.  It also ties to a lot in terms of ethics, so ethical considerations also need to be there, so that the quality of work is ok.  

Then, for water resource authority, I’ll say what has worked well, I can’t say well, but what is important to note is that most of these governments at the national level are mainly operating as a national entity, so it’s more of you approaching them.  If you expect a government officer to come and tell you that “I hear KIWASH is here, I’m with documentation for you”, it’s not going to happen.  So, it’s more of you going to the offices and asking about certain projects, and getting documentation.  And it requires a lot of initiative, because you find that something like a water obstruction permit for a borehole, for a water project to apply, requires like 7 types of documentation.  So it requires the borehole obstruction permit from the contractor, it requires the water quality analysis, it requires like a mapping of the area where you want to supply the water, it requires a license if that is an area that is covered by maybe another water supply , so it requires a lot of documentation before you even get that permit to obstruct.  So if you find that community doesn’t have that permit, you’re really required to show them like “have you gotten these“, and most of them didn’t have any kind of those documents and that’s why you’ll find that government officers, they’ll just sit because they know if they start following up on these things it’s going to be a challenge for them.  So if you select the projects which are serious, you know sometimes as a project you have a target, so you still have to maintain certain targets maybe for training, you don’t say like “these guys aren’t serious, we’re not going to train them” but you know when it comes to anything that is past that, you now focus on the 3 or 4 which you know that they are also going to give you good results.  Because it requires a lot of work.  You can’t go and tell guys like “you require this documentation, go and visit these offices” and then come after a week or two and guys are still sitting there.  So if you work with good projects, and guys who have initiative, they will work with you all the way and they’ll get these documentation.  And if you get these documentations to the government officers they have no problem issuing the permits, but if they don’t have the documentation, even for them it’s difficult because it’s a requirement.  

And then with Caritas, there’s a lot of engagement, because I think what they’ve developed is important, and something worth looking at in the future.  And it links to this because training is one component but the other is managing rural water schemes, because this is more a of a management contract model – the maintenance service provision - so its more of a PPP.  But a community project can pair with a WSP (water service provider) like KITWASCO to supply – that’s another model.  in its self it can be a good project and you can work on it so it gets proper documentation and it becomes like a small water supply.  So what I would say in terms of sustainability and management, going forward is not like one shoe fits all, it’s very dynamic.  You can’t say you’re going to apply one thing to every project, because they’re very dynamic.  They will go to a project and find that it is so unmanaged that you don’t need to bring in a private player, so you just work with them so they improve the way they’re working and get proper documentation to move forward, and then you’ll go to another and find that they don’t have the proper capacity, so you’ll need to bring in a private player to support them in one way or another.  You’ll find another one that is very close to a major water supply like KITWASCO, and if you just bring them together to make an agreement, then they’ll work together very well.  Since these guys (KITWASCO) are like a big company and they have a contract with them, and these guys (community project) can learn from these guys (KITWASCO) and get support, even maintenance and the rest.  So it depends on a project by project basis.   That’s why we are thinking of working with Caritas, such that we get to understand all these models so we can see … because KIWASH is very interested in seeing post-infrastructure support, how projects will be managed sustainably.    

Interviewer:  My second question is, “What do you think are the main problems in sustaining rural water services in the county?

Interviewee: I’ll start from the community aspect, I think I mentioned some of the challenges, but just generally maybe the lack of initiative or you’ll find a community that is not serious.  They just want to do things the way they were doing them before.  There’s a lot of ethical issues that are there, there’s a lot of mismanagement, there’s a lot of not understanding where the money is going, they don’t want to conform to certain requirements or regulations.  So that can be a challenge – it’s not easy to work with such a project.

Some of them have hardware challenges – they want to grow and provide water, but they have serious hardware challenges.  For example, maybe heavy rains washed away their intake.  So some of them, the challenges of hardware is difficult to come up with a solution unless the county can come in and support them.  

There’s the challenge of records – collecting money, how much water they’re producing and losing, how much are they paying their staff, and for electricity, how much money they’re getting in terms of allowances, because sometimes they pay themselves a lot of allowances – the management.  Like they meet for 2 or 3 times a week, and you’ll find a committee that has 10 – like sorry to say, but maybe it’s a Kenyan thing – you know Kenyans, we like positions, so you’ll find a water scheme maybe has two kiosks and a management committee of 12 members (laughs): there’s a chairman, vice chairman, treasurer, vice treasurer, a secretary, vice secretary and five members, and they meet on a weekly basis (laughing).  And maybe every time they meet, they get like 200 or 300 or 400 (Kenyan shillings) each from the project in terms of allowances, so most of the money will just be lost in form of allowances for those meetings.  So that’s also a challenge in management.  So there’s a financial aspect, there’s a governance aspect and there’s also a hardware/physical aspect.

Then county (govt) challenge is if you find a lack of support because of different approaches.  You find that we have an MOU, but an MOU is not a very legally binding document – so its more of them taking initiative on a personal level.  Of course you can’t mention names, but I find that a certain sub-county, it becomes very hard to work in that sub-county because of a certain sub-county officer or an official, just because they don’t give you the support that you require.  But you’ll find there’s another one that gives you a lot of support.  And there’s nothing much you can do, because you don’t have any authority or influence in such kind of situation.  So the challenge is mainly that since you don’t have influence and authority, and since they have their own targets to cover under the county itself, unless you find that there are those officials who really want to improve and understand that if they work with KIWASH its also an improvement to their work, then it becomes easier – but the ones who don’t want to work with you, there isn’t much you can do – unless you go directly to the community, or you find there are juniors, which sometimes brings conflict between them.  The young engineers – you know at the sub-county, there’s the sub-county officers and also the junior officers – so you may find when you go in the field, you find a young officer who is really still passionate and really supports you.  So sometimes that’s a strategy we use.  If you don’t find someone at a higher level who supports you then maybe find someone at a lower level who will support you.  And if the person above tries to disrupt that kind of relationship, it becomes difficult because you’re also working above him from a headquarter level.  It’s not a KIWASH only challenge – we find that at the county also it’s a challenge for them.  The county itself we also support because we find that they also don’t have enough resources, that is both the physical resources and the monitoring resources, so when you engage a county officer, you have to consider how you’ll facilitate them – whether its by using a KIWASH vehicle, or you give them some allowance for transport, so that is also challenge.  Because county also has limited resources.  

One thing I should mention that I forgot under success is under the county, is what we call pump testing.  The county has bought two rigs and two pump testing equipment trucks, so that has helped instead of KIWASH going through the whole procurement, you know procurement is messy when it comes to procuring someone to do something it can take even 3 months or maybe more, so now we work with them and they tell us we can use their machines ask us to give the staff, 4 or 5 guys, an allowance, and because it has to be done 24 hrs, a night allowance. And also we provide fuel for the machine.  And then there’s no other cost we incur.  So it has also worked very well and they still do a good job.  We do a bit of supervision to ensure that it’s done well as KIWASH, but its also a success.  So I think its something that can be adopted in terms of NGOs working in the field directly with the county in terms of the resources the county has.  It's a just a matter of understanding what they would require for you to work together – because its mainly facilitation.  It’s not like sometimes they don’t want to help, but that they don’t have the money to do that, so if we can come up with a way, then they really appreciate that.

For KITWASCO and KIMWASCO, they’re actually in two different aspects.  And sometimes I’ll talk maybe personally, I don’t know if it’s… but the way I’ve been hearing things, KIMWASCO is being managed very well actually – the staff that are there, the management that is there.  That is Mwingi water supplies, that is being managed very well.  It has its own challenges you know the problem with this area, the only water resource that is there is ground water.  Most of the surface water is not enough and it is seasonal.  So most of the water is groundwater. And for a town like Mwingi, it requires a lot of water. So they have a few groundwater sources, but most of the water they get is from Kiambere dam, which is actually in another county, it’s a bit far.   So they need to manage that scheme and it’s a huge scheme, and they’re managing it very well because of just very good guys who are employed.  You’ll find that the technical manager is an engineer, the MD is and experienced person and is also and engineer.  And the technical guys are good technical guys who can properly manage a scheme.  From the initiatives we have done, we’re finding that they’re even better to work with.  We’re even supporting them to do a second source from a river that is close by.  So that is also working very well because of the management that is there, in terms of professionality, and qualifications of the staff.  And there isn’t a lot of political influence from that side.  But now from this side, KITWASCO, because it’s managed in Kitui,  there is a lot of political influence, and it has also affected the management of the company unfortunately, and it’s also affecting its performance, so that is a challenge.  And it comes in terms of the people who are employed there, sometimes it’s not based on merit but based on political influence or political considerations, so that affects the kind of management that is there and it also affects the company.  So that’s a challenge.

Interviewer:  And my third question is “What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems?”

Interviewee: I think most of the solutions in the water sector are known, but it’s commitment and implementation that is becoming a challenge in this country.  For community schemes, it’s pretty straight forward it’s what Fundifix and you guys are doing in terms of maintenance and trying to bring in the private sector and looking at how they can be better managed.  That would work well because the current system isn’t working.  It has not worked for quite some while.  It’s a matter of looking at how to incorporate professionalism in this management.  This is both in terms of private sector involvement or better supervision and regulation - that is through the county, or any other actors.  But there need to be systems that manage these projects better.  But there aren’t systems it becomes very hard to hold any person accountable to anything.  But if these projects are properly and professionally set up and the systems are there, then you find that it even becomes difficult for mismanagement of the resources.

Sometimes I even give examples during trainings of two sectors in Kenya: the water sector and the education sector.  If you look at the education sector at the primary or secondary school level, a school is managed a number of institutions.  It is managed by the PTA, that is a Parents-Teachers Association, it is managed by the head teacher’s association or the head teacher himself and it’s also managed by the government itself under the ministry, that is the district level education officer or a certain official from the government.  I’m not saying they’re the best but education is doing better than the water sector.  Because if this is a government school, if it’s mismanaged, you are liable as the head teacher, you are liable as the district education officer, and even parents themselves can complain to a certain association or a certain entity.  But if we come to the water sector, its only managed my one aspect, which is the community, which is like the parents association only.  So there isn’t a good structure.  And for the education sector, this is in law, it is supposed to be there – that is for a government school.  But for water there isn’t a very good structure – the sub-county or county official yes, they are supposed to supervise and ensure that these are done well– but is it really entrenched in their legislation? It is not.  The management committee has fully autonomy over everything that is being done in that project. They can withdraw funds, they can pay the way they want, they can do almost anything.  So unless there is proper systems and legislation that is put in place, so that all these players come in: the community that is there, the county that has the oversight because the community cannot manage, and then there is also someone like a teacher or someone who is professionally trained – at that level it doesn’t have to be a degree person or a diploma person, it can be a certificate, like there is a project here that is being managed by a young lady that has done a certificate at KEWI (Kenya Water Institute), a 6 month or 1 year certificate on how to manage water resources.  But that person now is a professional managing that project and everyone has their own mandate and autonomy, and it’s structured under a certain documentation or policy or legislation that everyone is liable in a certain way and has responsibilities.  Because if you expect certain guys from the community to manage the water schemes, its not working.  That’s the solution I think for that.

At the county level, I think it requires more supervision and monitoring for these schemes.  That is lacking,  I don’t know if its because of a lack of resources or structure that are in place, but that is required.  They need to be on the ground and checking these schemes, having proper records of how they are performing.  Because right now if you got to a sub-county official and ask them,  even asking them “how many projects are in the sub county?”, “how many are functional? How many are non functional?” “the ones that are functional how are they performing?”, they don’t have that information.  So that kind of monitoring is not there.  I know its also because now this constitution came, we’re just changing from one system to another, and we’re still working on how to improve.  But its been 8 years, we need to start doing things different on that.

Yeah, also when it comes to the companies (KITWASCO, KIMWASCO; water service providers), I think you can’t remove the political aspect in the companies because water is also political and a social good, so it also has a lot of political influence, but I think we can try to remove the negative aspect of it.  Put people who are professional in managing these companies and they are liable in terms of performance, and we’re able to see even at the county if they are working well and how they are performing.  Because documentation is there in terms of policies, strategies are there for these companies.  They’re even developing some, we have given them some.  But enforcement and how can I call it – don’t call it ownership, but it’s more of commitment yes, commitment both from political officers or from the company management itself is a bit lacking you know.  It’s a challenge not just for Kitui, it’s cross-cutting in Kenya, the lack of commitment.  We may do very good documentation, but achieving and implementing and commitment is not there.   If that improves we’ll be able to see some changes in those kinds of companies.

There’s also a linkage with issues to do with tariff.  I know it’s a challenge for them. Sometimes they cannot adjust that tariff because its also  a social good and people need to afford water.  But I know there’s legislation and there’s a way they can come to a kind of understanding how.  Maybe a structured tariff, or pro-poor tariff, or reducing their non-revenue water.  All those things they know have been documented, they know what they are supposed to do.  But commitment and somebody to drive those initiatives is lacking.  We have good case studies or examples in this country of companies which have turned around in a period of even less than 3 or 4 years just because of good guys in management who are committed to improving service delivery.
