
1.In your opinion, what do you think are the main problems to Sanitation/water service sustainability in your woreda? Can you please explain in detail how these problems influence sanitation/water service sustainability?
Groundwater in this region contains high concentration of fluoride; this is the worst challenge we have in the region. Another difficulty is the way of life for the majority of the rural population: mobile in nature. Since they are pastoralists, it has created difficulties to provide them a long term sanitation and hygiene services. Majority of the people in this region rely on the main that drains most of the areas in the region: the Awash River. They are using that both for their animals and domestic chores. The water is not pure, because it is mainly from the Awash River. Occurrence of water-borne diseases like cholera is common in the area.
Scarcity of groundwater is also a challenge to the region. I can give you a practical example for this problem. If you travel from the center to Logiya along the way to Asayta, we have burrowed about 29 water wells but only one borehole is found to be free of fluoride. All other have high fluoride contents and we couldn’t continue with them since we don’t have a technology to purify once we make the water available for service.  
2.What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these problems? 
The regional WASH Coordination Office is conducting a study by being together with geologists. The project is expected to identify areas where pure water sources are found and design the process. We are also informed in a meeting that a machine will be installed soon so that we can start the process of purification. If this is working, we could solve almost the 50% of the problems we are facing this time.   
     Interviewer: What is the solution to the way of life of the people?
The government has launched a strategy to bring the different villages together and provide them basic facilities so that people could start living a sedentary way of life. Accordingly, the pastoralists can have access to pasture for their animals and services like education, water and health facilities for themselves. This project has its own independent project management located at the regional pastoral bureau to follow up the progress. 
The project office is doing this activity in closer collaboration with the regional education, water, health, and women and children bureaus. 
Interviewer: You said the community has lack of awareness of using water treatment chemicals. What is the solution to this?
There are 25 districts (weredas) along the Awash River basin. Of those woredas, some 16 are highly vulnerable to water and sanitation problems and exposed to water borne diseases. To this end, we are asking donors for help as it requires a coordinated action. We are asking CARE Ethiopia, AMREF and UNICEF to provide us with plastic buckets. While we don't know how many millions of people are really in danger, we are waiting for help and working on it. When there is a cholera outbreak, we go out to those identified areas and provide them with prevention techniques. We've already done that in one district. That is the only option we have.
Interviewer: What do you suggest a possible solution for cholera since it is common in Afar?
Though we continue to provide awareness, it has become hard to make a difference. People don't feel that it is possible. The main issue from the community is the problem of fluoride, and not cholera. The water looks clean, and that is why ordinary people don’t give attention to and use it for the different purposes. However, it has severe and long lasting effects on health. This year, we also spent a couple of months training the community not to use water from an underground water-well that has rich fluoride. We also go out to woredas to create awareness in the regular program, called community mobilization event. This is bringing a change. 
3.Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs
I think the important thing is working on community mobilization. We did a good job in 2006, and we reached a high standard of cleanliness and sanitation. We did successful jobs of cleaning up wells and doing public toilets through community mobilization program. Then they came up with a one to five correspondence but it was not functional. One-in- five group system does not help in the pastoral context. 
The second one that should be done sooner is mobilizing the high ranking officials and building their awareness. Third, the state and federal government should work closely to make the community members build a sense of ownership for the water schemes. Water service should benefit everyone. Our health workers are the ones most affected the burden of creating awareness on this issue. We have a team of pathologists; they are now in the field for purpose of provision of first aid/ medical services. Since a precautionary measure was not done, they spend much of their time out there in the communities doing prevention and treatment following the outbreak. There are 25 districts in this area, which are considered as hotspots, 16 of which have severe problems. Success can only be feasible when there is a true synergy among health, education and women and children bureaus.
