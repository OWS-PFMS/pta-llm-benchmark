Question 1: In your opinion, what do you think are the main challenges to water service sustainability in Kitui?  Could you please explain in detail how these challenges influence water service sustainability?
Respondent: I think that one of the challenges we are facing is project selection. The way in which we select projects is a challenge. Selecting a facility and getting people to own that facility is a challenge because the government won’t be there to maintain the systems the challenge is largely selection of facilities and getting buy in from the community for them to own that facility. 
The other challenge is our failure to address community priorities. Here I mean still anchoring to ownership and selection. You know, the community can tell you that ‘this is our need’ and then you may fail to address their need. Like now you may think that what is viable is a borehole from your technical view but the community would prefer an earth dam. You see, at that point they have not bought the idea of doing a borehole. If as a technical person you go ahead and drill a borehole, while aware that its maintenance is a challenge, the community will not own it or maintain it. These issues are intertwined.
Another issue is the lack of maintenance and operation practices by the people whom we are targeting. The government will not be there to do operation and maintenance and you know that some schemes may need local experts who can be the overseers of that project. You know we may have local operators but they are not paid. This is where outsourcing can come in handy and we can invest in building the capacity of the local operators such that if a system fails, they are able to respond in a timely manner. 
Question 2: What ideas or recommendations do you have about solutions to these challenges?
Respondent: In terms of ownership, you know, when a contractor is given a task to do, he only does his assignment and then he leaves. We may need to think about having the contractors do capacity building for a given time frame. Once contractors leave, the community just says that the facility is the contractors’ – they do not think that they are the ones to be in charge of maintaining and managing it. More investment needs to go into public participation to clearly identify what the community needs, to get community priorities even before the budgeting. W have to go to the community to get their priority interventions. It is easier for them to own projects once this procedure is followed otherwise organizations will be doing white elephant projects. The challenges will persist without this approach. You know, communities are key in this. I am also part of the community and when a facility is set up and it is not benefitting the target population and maintenance is not being done, the community will go back to drawing water from rivers where they normally fetch water.
A solution to the maintenance challenges can be anchored onto a policy guideline by the government which should give clear direction on how things must be done. So, we have to review the policy procedures and guidelines that we have within the county and ensure that they address operation and maintenance which is key across the rural water supplies. So, the main issues are operation and maintenance and how to tackle it and the lack of community capacity building on how to maintain facilities. We may call it indigenous knowledge which they are able to use by themselves apart from relying on the external support. The other issue may be the dependence on external support to manage and maintain their facilities; they are not able to mobilize among themselves. The external dependency is too much. 
Question 3: Of the solutions you listed, which is the most important? Can you walk me through what next steps would happen if the solution occurs?
Respondent: You know in every environment and even with organizations there are standard operating procedures that provide guidance on how matters should be handled. So, in my opinion, the policy guideline is most important. It will provide direction on project selection, community participation, handling of project technical aspects and such. It will also dictate procedures and structures for operation and maintenance, community mobilization, reduction of dependency on external support and project ownership in general. I other words, once a proper policy is in place, all other things will fall into place and sustainability will be achieved.
Question 4: What kind of interventions are you implementing in Kitui and what influenced/informed the selection of these interventions?
Respondent: What we are doing in terms of WASH in Kitui County, we have been in Kitui for the last 20 years targeting the rural areas and especially the marginalized areas. Specifically, we are doing borehole drilling and rehabilitation whereby we are targeting the Mwingi Central zones and then we are also constructing sand dams, earth dams, shallow wells, rock catchments, sump wells and then the farm ponds – these are focusing on surface water harvesting. We are supporting schools with masonry tanks and also promoting household access to water whereby we are supporting the component of village sales and loaning schemes whereby women groups come together and get loans to buy water tanks for each household. You see that integration bit of our programming – so, we are promoting access to credit. We are also doing community capacity building because our target is the rural areas whereby the challenges of maintenance are critical especially in all the schemes that we normally develop. Due to our focus on rural areas, we normally have the community water users’ associations which we normally form after we do site selection and mapping the water facility. Now this forms the committee which is mandated to do the coordination of the activity implementation, monitoring of the facility, and then after we are through with the facility, now we do the operation and maintenance training for them. That is what we have been doing, so we major in those three and the capacity building of the water users’ associations.
These are the broad strategies and they are informed by the community needs. We have a process we call the Participatory Vulnerability Capacity Assessment (PVCA). So, from this process we normally have a selection criterion for our target locations and we are also guided by the government poverty indices. We select the most affected community area and we have to mobilize these communities to understand their vulnerabilities, needs and capacities. We then enter the data into a template or a matrix where we analyze the vulnerabilities, their causes and come up with strategies to address the vulnerabilities. The strategies will be drawn from their suggestions.
Our work is largely informed by the PVCA methodology and experience or lessons learned from implementation of other projects. In the PVCA methodology, there is also a component of community resources mapping. The community draws a map of all their water resources, schools, market centres an settlement area. So, from this map, that is where we interrogate… “you have this river, what is your priority for this village?”. In terms of experience and lessons learned, we usually review all projects completed at the end of the year, as well as the recommendations from various consultants involved in the projects. This helps us decide our plans and the interventions we want to implement.
